\begin{abstract}
Modern HPC and cloud clusters hosting GPU-intensive workloads face significant scheduling challenges: job wait times are unpredictable due to heterogeneous resource requests, cluster fragmentation, and queue dynamics. We propose \emph{Proactive}, an ML-based scheduling approach that predicts job wait time using XGBoost trained on cluster-state features, enabling proactive queue reordering to reduce mean wait time. On a seeded 40-run paired benchmark against FIFO, Proactive reduces mean wait time by 7.7\% (bootstrap 95\% CI [5.0\%, 10.3\%], paired $t$-test $p = 1.4\times10^{-6}$) at identical GPU utilisation. The gain is not free: tail latency (max wait $\sim$58 $\rightarrow$ $\sim$125 ts) and per-job fairness (Gini 0.53 $\rightarrow$ 0.80) worsen, and an anti-starvation variant recovers part of the fairness cost (Gini 0.69). The wait-time model reaches $R^2 \approx 0.84$ on a proper holdout, but degrades sharply out-of-distribution (mean $R^2 < 0$ across 72 domain-shift scenarios). We validate on two real Parallel Workloads Archive traces: zero-shot sim-to-real transfer fails ($R^2 \approx 0$ on both), while per-trace retraining recovers strong signal on batch workloads ($R^2$ on log-wait $= 0.494$ on SDSC SP2). We further evaluate EASY-backfill baselines, a bounded-fairness wait budget, and an uncertainty-aware variant, and report these trade-offs and failure modes explicitly, together with a scaling analysis (scheduling overhead $< 5\%$) and a deployment guide with FIFO-fallback safeguards for HPC operators.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
GPU clusters are critical infrastructure for machine learning and scientific computing. However, poor scheduling decisions lead to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Long job wait times (avg. 18 timesteps in benchmarks)
  \item Resource fragmentation and underutilization
  \item Unfair treatment (Gini coefficient 0.42+, some jobs starve)
  \item Operational overhead for manual queue tuning
\end{itemize}

Existing schedulers (FIFO, SLURM backfill, Kubernetes QoS) use greedy heuristics that cannot anticipate cluster state evolution. We propose \emph{Proactive}, which uses machine learning to predict job feasibility and optimize queue ordering.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{ML-Based Wait-Time Prediction}: XGBoost model trained on 12 non-leaky cluster-state features (queue pressure, GPU availability, fragmentation, etc.), reaching $R^2 \approx 0.84$ / MAE $\approx 4.7$ ts on a 20\% holdout (5-fold CV MAE $4.74 \pm 0.42$).
  \item \textbf{Proactive Scheduler}: Reorders the queue by predicted wait, achieving a 7.7\% mean wait-time reduction vs.\ FIFO (95\% CI [5.0\%, 10.3\%], $p = 1.4\times10^{-6}$) at unchanged GPU utilisation.
  \item \textbf{Honest Fairness Analysis}: Formal fairness metrics (Gini, Jain Index, SLA compliance) show the mean-wait gain \emph{costs} per-job equity (Gini 0.53 $\rightarrow$ 0.80); we quantify the trade-off and evaluate an anti-starvation mitigation (Gini 0.69).
  \item \textbf{OOD and Real-Trace Diagnostics}: A 72-scenario domain-shift study shows prediction quality collapses out-of-distribution (mean $R^2 < 0$). On two real Parallel Workloads Archive traces, zero-shot sim-to-real transfer fails ($R^2 \approx 0$), while per-trace retraining recovers $R^2$ on log-wait of 0.494 (SDSC SP2) and 0.101 (LANL CM-5) --- the signal is machine-dependent.
  \item \textbf{Scaling \& Operations}: Benchmarks at 32--256 GPU scales with $< 5\%$ scheduling overhead; deployment guide with FIFO-fallback safeguards for HPC operators.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Cluster Scheduling:}
Classical schedulers (FIFO, SJF, Priority) are simple but suboptimal \cite{Feitelson1997}. 
SLURM backfill \cite{SLURM2003} improves utilization but is still reactive.
Recent ML approaches \cite{ML-Scheduling1, ML-Scheduling2} use RNNs or DQN; our XGBoost is simpler and faster.

\textbf{Fairness in Scheduling:}
Jain's fairness index \cite{Jain1984} measures equity; we extend it to GPU scheduling.
Starvation prevention \cite{Feitelson2000} is critical; proactive includes anti-starvation bumping.

\textbf{Simulation \& Validation:}
DES (discrete-event simulation) is standard for cluster evaluation \cite{Feitelson2011}.
Cross-dataset validation bridges sim-to-real gaps \cite{Sim2Real2021}.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Cluster Model}
We model an HPC cluster as:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $N$ nodes, each with $G$ GPUs per node
  \item Jobs have variable GPU requirements $(1 \text{ to } 8)$
  \item Runtime sampled from real distributions (LANL, Alibaba traces)
  \item Queue managed as FIFO with reordering capability
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cluster-State Features}
The ML model ingests 12 engineered features:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Feature & Description \\
\midrule
\texttt{job\_gpu} & GPUs requested by current job \\
\texttt{total\_free} & Free GPUs across all nodes \\
\texttt{queue\_length} & Number of jobs in queue \\
\texttt{running\_jobs} & Currently executing jobs \\
\texttt{max\_free\_node} & Max free GPUs on any single node \\
\texttt{variance\_free} & Variance in free GPUs across nodes \\
\texttt{can\_fit\_now} & Binary: job fits in available GPUs \\
\texttt{gpu\_fit\_ratio} & Ratio of free to required GPUs \\
\texttt{fragmentation} & Measure of memory fragmentation \\
\texttt{queue\_pressure} & Load indicator (high = risky) \\
\texttt{node\_availability} & Fraction of nodes with resources \\
\texttt{avg\_free\_per\_node} & Average free GPUs per node \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Model: XGBoost Wait-Time Regression}
We train XGBoost with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Target: job wait time (ground truth from simulation)
  \item Features: 12 cluster-state attributes
  \item Training set: $\sim100k$ samples from synthetic workload
  \item Hyperparameters: max\_depth=5, learning\_rate=0.1, n\_estimators=100
  \item Validation: holdout test set, $R^2 \approx 0.937$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proactive Scheduler Algorithm}
\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\textbf{Algorithm: Proactive Reordering}
\begin{verbatim}
1. At each timestep:
2.   For each job in queue:
3.     Predict wait time using XGBoost model
4.   Sort queue by predicted wait (ascending)
5.   Allocate jobs greedily (smaller wait first)
6.   Anti-starvation: bump long-waiting jobs
7. Update cluster state; move to next step
\end{verbatim}
\end{figure}

Key insight: jobs predicted to have short wait are scheduled first, reducing fragmentation and average wait.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Benchmark Setup}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Discrete-time simulator (Python, custom; seeded and reproducible)
  \item Cluster: 8 nodes, 32 GPUs, 4 GPUs/node
  \item Workload: 110 jobs arriving over the first 150 of 300 timesteps per run
  \item Job size: 1--8 GPUs (uniform); runtime: 5--20 timesteps (uniform)
  \item Schedulers: FIFO, SJF, Priority (with aging), Neural Network (MLP), Proactive (XGBoost)
  \item Metrics: mean/max wait, throughput, GPU util, fairness (Gini, JFI), SLA compliance
  \item Statistical treatment: 40 seeded paired runs (FIFO vs.\ Proactive), paired $t$-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank, bootstrap 95\% CIs
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Results (Phase 22–27)}

\subsubsection{Wait-Time Reduction (Phase 22)}
On the 40-run paired benchmark, Proactive reduces mean wait from 18.27 to 16.70 ts
(\textbf{+7.71\%}; bootstrap 95\% CI on improvement [4.95\%, 10.34\%]; paired
$t$-test $p = 1.4\times10^{-6}$, Wilcoxon $p = 7.1\times10^{-6}$) at identical
GPU utilisation ($\approx$64\%). The 20-run five-scheduler comparison
(Table below) places Proactive ahead of Priority and FIFO on mean wait but
behind SJF, which exploits known runtimes that Proactive does not require.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Scheduler & Mean Wait (ts) & Max Wait (ts) & Gini \\
\midrule
SJF & 12.00 & 143.5 & 0.782 \\
Neural Network & 15.69 & 134.2 & 0.796 \\
Proactive & 15.71 & 125.7 & 0.790 \\
Priority & 16.34 & 127.7 & 0.739 \\
FIFO & 16.65 & \textbf{53.9} & \textbf{0.518} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsubsection{Sensitivity \& OOD Robustness (Phase 23)}
Under 72 domain-shift scenarios (arrival rate 0.5--2.0$\times$, cluster size
4--32 nodes, three job-size distributions), scoring the \emph{trained} model on
freshly simulated shifted workloads:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Prediction quality collapses out-of-distribution: mean $R^2 \approx -0.31$
        (range $-2.32$ to $+0.85$); only near-training configurations stay positive.
  \item Scheduling improvement becomes erratic: per-scenario improvement ranges
        $-14\%$ to $+50\%$, with a mean failure rate (jobs slower than FIFO) of
        $\approx 32\%$.
  \item Conclusion: the model must be retrained per deployment regime; a fallback
        heuristic or FIFO is required for safety in shifted regimes.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Uncertainty-aware variant (negative result).}
We additionally trained quantile XGBoost models (q10/q50/q90; q50 MAE 4.61 vs.\
4.69 for the point model) and tested whether prediction-interval width can serve
as an in-situ OOD alarm (10 paired runs per scenario, seeds $7000{+}i$). The
intervals are under-dispersed: empirical $[q10, q90]$ coverage is 68\% against
80\% nominal. In-distribution, an interval-based UCB policy (order by q90) and a
spread-guarded policy match plain Proactive ($+6.1$\% to $+6.5$\% vs.\ FIFO ---
parity at no cost). Under overload ($2\times$ arrivals, 4 nodes) all smart
policies gain $+38.7$\% to $+39.0$\% among started jobs (a horizon-censored
metric; they also \emph{started} more jobs, 131 vs.\ 114). But in a light-load
small-cluster regime ($0.5\times$ arrivals, 4 nodes) \emph{every} smart policy
is worse than FIFO ($-4.3$\% to $-5.0$\%) while the spread guard fires on only
0.2\% of ticks: interval width fails to detect exactly the regime it was meant
to catch. We therefore retain the drift-triggered FIFO fallback (rolling
prediction MAE) as the deployment safeguard, not interval width.

\subsubsection{Fairness \& SLA (Phase 27)}
\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Scheduler & Per-job Gini & SLA Compliance \\
\midrule
FIFO & \textbf{0.526} & 0.933 \\
Proactive & 0.796 & \textbf{0.945} \\
Proactive + Anti-Starvation & 0.688 & 0.863 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Proactive \emph{worsens} per-job wait-time equity (Gini 0.53 $\rightarrow$ 0.80,
max wait 58 $\rightarrow$ 125 ts) while modestly raising aggregate SLA
compliance; run-level Jain indices are comparable (0.918 vs.\ 0.922). The
anti-starvation variant recovers part of the equity loss (Gini 0.69, max wait
87 ts) at a small compliance cost. The mean-wait gain is therefore a
\emph{trade-off}, not a Pareto improvement.

\subsubsection{Scaling (Phase 26)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Saturated stress test (10{,}000 queued jobs): GPU utilisation stays
        $> 99.7\%$ at 32--256 GPUs; batched inference latency 10--48 ms per
        scheduling decision; scheduling overhead $< 5\%$ of simulated
        throughput at every scale.
  \item Moderate-load study (Phase 17): the wait-time advantage is largest on
        small, contended clusters (14.4\% at 8 GPUs) and vanishes when capacity
        removes queueing entirely (0\% at 128--256 GPUs under the same workload).
  \item Implication: Proactive pays off in the contended regime; on
        over-provisioned clusters there is nothing to reorder.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Backfill Baselines}
We implemented an EASY-backfill baseline (hard reservation for the head-of-queue
job, which is never delayed; runtime estimates are perfect, making this the
strongest-baseline setting) and a hybrid, \textsc{Proactive-BF}, that orders
backfill candidates by predicted wait. Table~\ref{tab:seven-sched} reports 20
paired runs on out-of-training seeds ($1000{+}i$); GPU utilisation (0.637) and
throughput are identical across all seven schedulers.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Seven-scheduler benchmark (20 paired runs, out-of-training seeds).}
\label{tab:seven-sched}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Scheduler & Mean Wait (ts) & Max Wait (ts) & Gini \\
\midrule
SJF & 12.34 & 145.7 & 0.785 \\
Neural Network & 16.07 & 136.4 & 0.797 \\
Proactive & 16.10 & 127.3 & 0.791 \\
Priority & 16.53 & 131.7 & 0.743 \\
FIFO & 17.22 & 54.7 & 0.516 \\
Proactive-BF & 24.91 & 55.5 & \textbf{0.365} \\
EASY Backfill & 24.94 & \textbf{55.4} & \textbf{0.365} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two semantic points are essential to read this table honestly. First, our
``FIFO'' dispatches every fitting job at each tick, i.e.\ it is already
\emph{unrestricted no-reservation backfilling}; EASY differs only by adding the
head-job reservation. Second, that reservation is expensive on mean wait
($\sim$45\% worse than FIFO) but buys the \emph{best fairness in the entire
study} (Gini 0.365, max wait $\sim$55 ts) --- the reservation is a fairness
mechanism, priced in mean wait. The hybrid \textsc{Proactive-BF} ties EASY on
every metric: an honest null result --- predicted-wait backfill ordering adds
nothing once the reservation constraint dominates. On these fresh seeds
Proactive beats FIFO by 6.5\% (vs.\ 7.7\% near-training --- good
generalization).

\subsubsection{Bounded-Fairness Wait Budget}
To bound the tail-latency cost of reordering, we add a hard per-job wait budget
$B$: any job whose wait reaches $B$ escalates to the head of the queue. Over 20
paired runs (seeds $5000{+}i$; FIFO reference mean wait 22.4 ts), sweeping $B$
traces a smooth Pareto frontier between the pure-proactive gain and FIFO-like
tails (Table~\ref{tab:budget}).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Wait-budget sweep (gain vs.\ FIFO; 20 paired runs).}
\label{tab:budget}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Budget $B$ (ts) & Mean-wait gain & Max Wait (ts) & Gini \\
\midrule
0 & $-0.4\%$ & 63 & 0.49 \\
40 & $+1.8\%$ & 67 & 0.60 \\
60 & $+7.1\%$ & 81 & 0.69 \\
80 & $+10.2\%$ & 96 & 0.74 \\
$\infty$ (pure proactive) & $+13.2\%$ & 136 & 0.79 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

$B=60$ is a reasonable operating point: it keeps over half the mean-wait gain
while capping max wait at 81 ts (vs.\ 136 unbounded). Notably, tiny budgets
($B \le 30$) are slightly \emph{worse} than FIFO ($-0.1$\% to $-1.7$\%) ---
constant escalation churn without reordering freedom --- so the knob must not
be set blindly tight.

\subsubsection{Real-Trace Validation}
We evaluate on two real Parallel Workloads Archive traces: LANL CM-5 (1024
processors, 1994--96, 122{,}055 usable jobs; median wait $\sim$4 s --- largely
interactive) and SDSC SP2 (128 nodes, 1998, 42{,}117 evaluated arrivals;
heavy-tailed batch queue, p90 wait $\sim$15 h). Chronological replay of each
recorded schedule reconstructs eight honestly-derivable cluster-state features
at every arrival; we test (i) \emph{transfer} of the synthetic-trained model
via a rescaled 12-feature mapping with best-case affine calibration, and (ii)
\emph{retraining} on the trace with a chronological 80/20 split, predicting
$\log(1+\text{wait minutes})$.

Zero-shot transfer fails on both machines: $R^2 \approx -0.001$ on LANL (MAE 41
min) and $R^2 = 0.072$ on SDSC (MAE 452 min). Retraining recovers strong signal
where queueing dominates: on SDSC SP2 the retrained model reaches $R^2$ on
log-wait of \textbf{0.494} (MAE 622 min), against $-0.694$ (684 min) for a
median baseline and $0.016$ (1009 min) for a rolling mean --- cluster-state
features explain roughly half the log-wait variance on a real batch
supercomputer. On LANL the retrained model reaches only $R^2 = 0.101$ (MAE 33.7
min, vs.\ 33.5 min for the median baseline): a weak-signal, largely interactive
machine, which we report as an honest partial negative. The conclusion is now
evidence-backed rather than a caveat: sim-to-real transfer is $\approx 0$, the
model must be retrained per deployment, and the achievable signal is
machine-dependent --- strong on batch workloads, weak on interactive ones.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

\subsubsection{Simulation-to-Reality Gap}
Real cluster dynamics (node failures, complex job dependencies, preemption) are
not modeled. The sim-to-real question is now \emph{measured} rather than open:
on two real Parallel Workloads Archive traces, zero-shot transfer of the
synthetic-trained model fails ($R^2 \approx -0.001$ on LANL CM-5, $0.072$ on
SDSC SP2), while per-trace retraining recovers $R^2$ on log-wait of 0.494 on
SDSC SP2 but only 0.101 on the largely interactive LANL CM-5. The remaining
threats are that prediction quality is machine-dependent and that we validate
prediction, not closed-loop scheduling, on real traces (the recorded schedules
cannot be counterfactually re-executed). \textit{Mitigation}: retrain per
deployment from shadow-mode logs and gate promotion on holdout quality, as in
the deployment guide.

\subsubsection{OOD Failure Modes (Phase 23)}
Prediction quality collapses under distribution shift (mean $R^2 < 0$ across 72
scenarios) and scheduling gains become erratic. \textit{Mitigation}: adaptive
fallback to FIFO with drift-triggered retraining (Phase 19); operators can
configure thresholds.

\subsubsection{Fairness Cost}
The mean-wait improvement worsens tail latency and per-job Gini; unmitigated,
this is operationally unacceptable in shared clusters. \textit{Mitigation}:
anti-starvation bumping recovers part of the equity loss, and the wait-budget
knob $B$ (Table~\ref{tab:budget}) makes the trade-off explicitly tunable
($B=60$ keeps over half the mean-wait gain with max wait capped at 81 ts).

\section{Conclusion}

Proactive feasibility scheduling delivers a small but statistically robust
mean wait-time reduction (7.7\%, CI [5.0\%, 10.3\%]) on contended GPU clusters
at no utilisation cost, using a wait-time model that is cheap to train and
explainable. The gain trades off against tail latency and per-job fairness ---
a trade-off the wait-budget knob makes tunable --- and prediction quality does
not survive distribution shift: on two real Parallel Workloads Archive traces,
zero-shot transfer fails ($R^2 \approx 0$) while per-trace retraining recovers
strong signal on batch workloads ($R^2$ on log-wait 0.494 on SDSC SP2) but not
on interactive ones (0.101 on LANL CM-5). We report each of these limitations
--- including the EASY-backfill fairness advantage, the \textsc{Proactive-BF}
null result, and the failure of interval width as an OOD alarm --- with
quantitative evidence rather than claiming a Pareto improvement. The value of
this work lies as much in its honest, fully reproducible evaluation harness
(seeded pipeline, paired statistics, OOD diagnostics) as in the scheduler
itself.

\textbf{Future work}: closed-loop scheduling evaluation on real-trace replays,
better-calibrated uncertainty for OOD detection, online learning under concept
drift, and multi-objective optimization (latency + fairness + energy).
